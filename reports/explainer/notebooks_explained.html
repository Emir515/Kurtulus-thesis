Master's thesis · MetroAT · Vienna metro

The thesis notebooks, step by step

A Vienna metro train recorded about 100 sensors every second for a year and broke down 21 times. The thesis asks whether grouping the sensors by the part of the train they belong to helps predict breakdowns with fewer false alarms. Each notebook below does one part of that work.

Looking at the data01_data_exploration.ipynb

NB01 · Explore the raw data

Question: What is in the data, and what happens to the sensors when the train breaks down?

Steps

  1. Open the raw files: one row per second, about 22 million rows and 101 sensors.
  2. Find the breakdowns and the maintenance days in the data.
  3. Draw the sensors over the whole year.

Experiments and comparisons

  • Breakdown vs normal: which sensors change most during a breakdown? The spring-brake pressure of car CW1 changes most.
  • Same group vs other groups: sensors of the same part of the train move together more than sensors of different parts (checked with a shuffle test).

Outputs and how to read them

  • Breakdown and maintenance timelinePlotHow to read: the year runs left to right; each coloured bar is one recorded breakdown (by type) or one maintenance stay.What it shows: 21 breakdowns that come in waves: late summer and autumn 2024, then February to May 2025. 10 fall before the train/test split and 11 after it, although the test period is only about 4.5 months long. Maintenance happens roughly every 4 weeks (18 stays).Why it matters: The test months have more breakdowns per month than the training months, so the share of breakdown pieces is higher there (2.9% vs 1.3%). Every later comparison therefore uses a baseline from the same period.
  • Key sensors over the yearPlotHow to read: hourly averages of a few important sensors; flat bands are normal operation, white gaps are days without data.What it shows: Levels are steady through 2024 and shift in 2025.Why it matters: The first visual hint of the 2025 change (drift) that NB03, NB04 and the air-system analysis in NB05 then measure.
  • Normal vs breakdown distributionsPlotHow to read: for each sensor two histograms of the raw 1-second values (training months, maintenance removed): normal operation and inside a recorded breakdown. The further apart, the larger the effect (Cohen’s d).What it shows: The largest shifts are on the CW1 side: spring-brake pressure d = −1.5, main air-tank pressure −1.1 and load pressure −1.1, all lower during breakdowns.Why it matters: During breakdowns air pressure drops on the CW1 side. These sensors come back in the groups (FC7: CW1 spring-brake pressure, FC12: main air tanks).Careful: These are values during a breakdown, not before it, so they say nothing yet about prediction.
  • Correlation matrix, ordered by documented sensor groupPlotHow to read: a grid of all sensor pairs, sorted by the sensor groups in the MetroAT documentation; darker means the two sensors move together more strongly.What it shows: Average strength inside the documented groups: energy braking 0.98, brake 0.72, leveling 0.35, air supply 0.23; between groups only 0.10 to 0.26. A shuffle test gives p = 0.001.Why it matters: The structure of the train is visible in the data, which supports the idea of subsystem-aware features.Careful: Not every group is tight: the air-supply sensors mix on/off signals with pressures and correlate weakly even among themselves.

What the results mean

  • 21 breakdowns, 13 of them brake: brake problems dominate. With only 2 height-control breakdowns, nothing reliable can be said about that type.
  • 10 training and 11 test breakdowns: every later result rests on few events, so one breakdown more or less can change a number noticeably.
  • Spring-brake pressure changes most: the spring brakes react strongly when something fails. That is why spring-brake sensors reappear in the reliable groups FC7 and FC8.
  • Sensors of one train part move together: the train's structure is visible in the data, which supports the core idea of the thesis.

In short: the data is suitable for the question, but there are few breakdowns, so results need careful checking.

Preparing the data02a_dataset_creation.ipynb

NB02a · Build the datasets

Question: How do we turn one reading per second into something a computer can learn from?

Steps

  1. Cut the year into 6-minute pieces.
  2. Summarise each piece: lowest, highest and average value of each sensor, and how often on/off signals switch.
  3. Give each piece a label: does a breakdown start in the next 6.2 hours?
  4. Also cut 23-minute clips for the second model type (the autoencoder).
  5. Keep the official split: training months until 11 Feb 2025, test months after.

Experiments and comparisons

  • Our data vs Stefan's: labels match 100%, so the comparison with his work is fair.
  • His code vs his thesis text: the text says pieces during a breakdown were removed, the code keeps them. We follow the code, because it gives his published numbers.
  • Clip building aligned with his code: prepared and tested, rebuilt in Phase 2.

Outputs and how to read them

  • Dataset summaryTableHow to read: the counts the notebook prints after building the datasets.What it shows: 41,375 training pieces (533 positive, 1.3%) and 23,147 test pieces (669 positive, 2.9%), 340 values each; 7,845 + 1,962 normal 23-minute clips for training the autoencoder and 5,374 test clips. The parquet files are byte-identical to the earlier NB02 run.Why it matters: With 1 to 3% positives, accuracy is meaningless (always saying "no breakdown" scores 97 to 99%), so the thesis reports F1, precision, recall and false-alarm rate.

What the results mean

  • Labels match Stefan's 100%: any difference to his results comes from the method, never from the data.
  • Only 1.3% (training) and 2.9% (test) of pieces are positive: a model that always says "no breakdown" would be right about 98% of the time. That is why accuracy is useless here and the thesis uses F1, precision and recall.
  • Pieces during a breakdown count as positive: part of the task is spotting a breakdown that is already happening, which is easier than predicting it. This is a limitation to state in the thesis.

In short: 64,522 comparable pieces, built exactly like the earlier work, so the comparison in Phase 2 is fair.

Waiting for Phase 202b_baselines.ipynb

NB02b · The old models (baseline)

Question: How well do the models from earlier work predict breakdowns when they see all sensors at once?

Steps

  1. Random Forest: learns from labelled examples. Tries 432 settings and keeps the best.
  2. Autoencoder: learns what normal looks like and raises an alarm when it cannot reproduce a clip.
  3. Score every test piece, in three ways: raw, smoothed using the past only, and smoothed using past and future (Stefan's way).

Experiments and comparisons

  • Rebuilding Stefan's score of 0.21 (1.0 = perfect): raw scores give 0.13, his smoothing gives 0.17, a fair smoothing that only uses the past gives 0.11.
  • His smoothing looks into the future, which a real train could never do. So the honest baseline is about 0.11 to 0.13.

Outputs and how to read them

  • Rebuilding the published score (check run, not the notebook itself)TableHow to read: the same Random Forest settings as Helm, scored three ways on the test months up to 1 June.What it shows: Raw scores F1 = 0.126, Helm’s two-sided smoothing 0.165 (Helm: 0.206), causal smoothing that only looks back 0.109.Why it matters: Most of the gap between our 0.12 and the published 0.21 comes from smoothing that uses future scores, which a train in service cannot do. The honest baseline is about 0.11 to 0.13.
  • Score timelines and training curvePlotHow to read: alarm score of each model over the test months with the alarm line and breakdowns, and the autoencoder’s training curve.What it shows: Produced when the notebook runs in Phase 2.Why it matters: Will show where the old models raise alarms and how many of them are false.

What the results mean

  • 0.21 needs smoothing that looks into the future: the published number cannot be reached by a system running on a real train.
  • An honest score of about 0.11 to 0.13: of all alarms, only about 1 in 9 is correct, and most breakdown pieces are missed. This is the false-alarm problem from the proposal in numbers.
  • The bar for Phase 2: the subsystem models have to beat this honest score, measured in exactly the same way.

In short: the baseline is weaker than published, and the thesis compares against the honest version.

Looking at the data03_dataset_analysis.ipynb

NB03 · Explore the prepared datasets

Question: Do the training and test months look the same, and where do the old models raise alarms?

Steps

  1. Plot the old models' alarm scores over the whole year.
  2. Find long alarm stretches (several pieces in a row above the alarm line).
  3. Check which values separate breakdown from normal, in each period separately.

Experiments and comparisons

  • Training vs test months: the autoencoder's error on normal data rises from 0.54 to 0.77. The train behaves differently in 2025 (drift).
  • Long alarms vs real breakdowns: Random Forest 186 long alarms, 27 real; autoencoder 554, only 10 real.
  • Average vs total of a piece: almost the same information (0.89), so NB04 drops the totals.

  • Summary statistics: which ones carry the informationPlotHow to read: top: average correlation between the summary types (lowest, highest, average, total, switches, seconds on) over all sensors; bottom: six example sensors.What it shows: Average and total are nearly the same (0.89, median 0.97).   • Autoencoder error rises from 0.54 to 0.77: the model learned what "normal" looked like in 2024 and sees much of 2025 as unusual. That explains many of its false alarms.
  • Only 27 of 186 long Random Forest alarms are real: about 1 in 7. A maintenance team would soon stop trusting such alarms.
  • 234 of 340 values separate breakdown from normal in both periods: there is real information in the data, so a better way of using it can help.
  • Average and total carry the same information: removing the totals avoids counting the same thing twice.

In short: the signal is there, but false alarms and the change in 2025 are the two big problems, so every later comparison is made within one period.

Main analysis04_anomaly_detection_cluster_analysis.ipynb

NB04 · Find groups of sensors that misbehave together

Question: Which sensors go wrong at the same time, and how do these groups behave around breakdowns?

Steps

  1. Mark a piece as unusual when at least 5 values are far outside their normal range: 12,022 pieces.
  2. Keep only the training months for building the groups: 5,351 pieces. The test months stay hidden.
  3. Put sensor values that are usually unusual together into one group: 19 groups.
  4. Use the fixed groups on the whole year to study the 21 breakdowns.

Experiments and comparisons

  • Different "unusual" rules: 30 combinations of thresholds tried.
  • Two grouping methods: they agree on 87% of sensor pairs.
  • With vs without test months: using them gave 31 groups, partly made by 2025 behaviour; without them, 19 cleaner groups.
  • Before vs during breakdowns: no group warns days ahead; FC5 and FC7 warn a few hours ahead.
  • Breakdown days vs normal days: FC2 and FC6 are busier on breakdown days in both periods.
  • Shakier values before breakdowns? Only FC18, slightly (1.25×).
  • 2024 vs 2025: 9 groups are much busier in 2025 without breakdowns nearby.

Outputs and how to read them

  • How many values are unusual per piecePlotHow to read: left: histogram of the number of unusual values (|z| > 3) per piece, normal vs breakdown pieces; the green line is the rule "at least 5". Right: the same number over the year, red = unusual pieces.What it shows: Breakdown pieces have clearly more unusual values (long red tail). 12,022 of 64,522 pieces (18.6%) pass the rule. They contain 724 of the 1,202 breakdown pieces (60%). A constant band at about 11 unusual values runs through the whole year.Why it matters: Unusual pieces are linked to breakdowns, but 94% of them are not near one. The constant band probably reflects a recurring normal situation; FC1 (brakes held while standing, active all year) is a likely candidate.

The chosen setting (|z| > 3, at least 5) sits in the middle.Why it matters: The results do not depend on one lucky setting; the chosen one is a balanced compromise.
  • Monthly share of unusual piecesPlotHow to read: one line for all pieces, one for pieces far from any breakdown, month by month; dotted = split.What it shows: Far from breakdowns 10.9% of training pieces are unusual, 27.4% of test pieces, and 43% in June 2025.Why it matters: Something changes in 2025 that has nothing to do with single breakdowns. What the results mean

  • 12,022 unusual pieces contain 60% of the breakdown pieces: unusual behaviour is linked to breakdowns. But 94% of the unusual pieces are not near a breakdown, so "unusual" alone would give far too many alarms.
  • 19 groups, 87% agreement between two methods: the groups are not a side effect of one particular method.
  • 31 groups with the test months, 19 without: letting the test months help would have leaked information. The 19 groups are the honest ones.
  • No warning days ahead, FC5 and FC7 hours ahead: the realistic goal is a short-term warning. A few hours can still be enough to take a train out of service.
  • FC2 and FC6 busier on breakdown days in both periods: stable groups for recognising a breakdown, less for predicting it.
  • Only FC18 gets shakier before breakdowns: rising variation is not a general warning sign on this train.
  • 9 groups react to 2025: these groups may follow the changed behaviour of the train rather than breakdowns, so they need extra care.

In short: the computer found meaningful sensor groups on its own; they mostly recognise breakdowns, and two warn a few hours ahead. Every group with its features is described below. A simpler diagram of this notebook is on its own page.

Steps

  3. Check the decision on the 11 hidden test breakdowns.

  6. Prepare the report and the sheet for the experts.

Experiments and comparisons

  • Training vs hidden breakdowns: 15 groups pass on training, only 3 hold on the hidden ones: FC7, FC5, FC8.

What the results mean

  • 15 groups pass on training, 3 hold on the hidden breakdowns: most patterns do not carry over to new breakdowns. FC5, FC7 and FC8 are the most trustworthy candidates for the subsystem datasets.
  • The hidden-test check is strict: 42% of test pieces lie near a breakdown, so failing it does not prove a group useless. The experts make the final call.
  • FC2, FC6 and FC18 sit behind false alarms: they may be normal operating situations. If the experts confirm this, leaving them out could reduce false alarms, which is exactly what RQ2 tests.
  • Spring-brake pressure falling to about 0: a very strong deviation, but FC7 and FC8 fire mostly while the train stands. Because a spring brake is applied by releasing air, this can be the parking brake, for example a train parked because of the fault. Real pressure loss, sensor dropout or parking brake: the experts decide.
  • Only 2.4% of the old model's attention on the reliable groups: the old model mostly ignores the most trustworthy signals. This is a direct reason to try subsystem-aware features in Phase 2.
  • Air loss grows every month, compressor sides swap at the April 2025 repair: the 2025 change may be real wear and a repair, not random noise. A monitoring system should also watch slow trends over months.
  • No change in the air values in the last 3 days: the air system shows slow wear, not a countdown to each single breakdown.

In short: 3 of 19 groups are reliable, the old model barely uses them, and the train's air system wore out slowly over the year. All of this goes to the experts in a 13-page report.

The 19 sensor groups up close

Each group from NB04, with all its features and what happens to them when the group fires. The three groups that hold on the hidden test breakdowns come first. The descriptions say what the sensors measure; which part of the train a group belongs to is for the Wiener Linien experts to decide.

How to read the feature lists

  • Cars: CW1 and CW2 are the two end cars, MW1 to MW4 the four motor cars in between. CW1, MW1, MW2 form side A, CW2, MW3, MW4 side B. B1 / B2 is bogie 1 or 2 of a car.
  • Per 6 min: lowest, highest or average value in a 6-minute piece; for on/off signals the number of switches.
  • Brake-cylinder pressure: the air pressure that presses the brakes on. Spring-brake pressure: the air pressure that holds the spring (parking) brake off, so low pressure means the spring brake is on. Control-valve pressure and air-brake force: how hard the air brake is asked to brake and how hard it does.
  • Main air-tank pressure and compressor on/off: the air supply. Load pressure and load signal: pressure in the air suspension and the weight reading of a car.
  • std: how far the typical value during the group’s activity lies from normal, in normal standard deviations (+ higher, − lower).
